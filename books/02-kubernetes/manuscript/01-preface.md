# About This Book {-}

This book is the second volume of the DevOps Handbook Series and the Kubernetes counterpart to Docker for DevOps Engineers; it assumes you have that foundation or an equivalent working knowledge: you can write a Dockerfile, run containers with sensible resource limits, read a compose file, and explain what a registry and an image digest are. Kubernetes is where those skills compound. Every chapter moves deliberately from concept to command, and every concept is introduced only when the preceding machinery makes it meaningful. Reading about Kubernetes is pleasant; typing `kubectl` against a real cluster is what makes the knowledge permanent.

The book is organized in two parts so it can serve two reading modes at once. Part I, Kubernetes Essentials, covers the vocabulary and the mechanics: the mental model, cluster anatomy, cluster creation with `kind` and `kubeadm`, workloads, configuration, networking, storage, and packaging with Helm and Kustomize. Part II, Advanced Operations, is the working engineer's part: security and RBAC, scheduling and autoscaling, stateful workloads and operators, observability, GitOps delivery, and production operations such as upgrades, backups, and incident response. The parts are sequential; nothing in Part II assumes material outside Part I and the earlier chapters of Part II itself.

The content reflects the ecosystem as of October 2026. Facts were checked against Kubernetes v1.37, released in August 2026, with v1.35 and v1.36 still inside their support windows. The three-minor release policy shapes real advice throughout: clusters are expected to upgrade roughly every four months, deprecated APIs are removed on schedule, and the book flags features by stability level so you know what is safe to build on. Newer-still features such as scaling workloads to zero with the `HorizontalPodAutoscaler` are described as alpha when they are alpha, not presented as settled practice.

Three conventions run through the book. Commands you type in a shell appear in monospaced blocks, and manifest files are shown in full so you can paste them directly. Technical terms such as `Pod`, `Service`, and `StatefulSet` are kept in English and capitalized when they name an API object, matching how professionals speak. Notes marked as Best practice condense operational wisdom, while Watch out boxes flag the mistakes that cause the most incidents, from `OOMKilled` pods to forgotten `etcd` backups. Because the book targets practitioners, full labs appear in the five chapters where hands-on practice changes the most: cluster creation, workload lifecycle, security, observability, and operations. The appendices compress the rest into a desk reference you will keep open long after the first read.

Every chapter closes with a *See also* list, every internal reference is a live link, and the Part dividers, table of contents, and headings are navigable in all three editions - in the PDF the contents carry computed page numbers and the heading tree appears in the outline panel.
