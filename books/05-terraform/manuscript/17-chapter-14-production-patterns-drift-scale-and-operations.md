# Chapter 14 - Production Patterns: Drift, Scale, and Operations {#ch14}

## 14.1 Drift: Detect It, Decide It, Then Converge It

Drift is the theorem of Chapter 1's axioms: reality changes outside Terraform (consoles, autoscalers, teammates with laptops), so state and world diverge, and every plan silently re-proposes the configuration. What separates mature estates from folklore is that drift is *scheduled*, not discovered. The nightly drift job from [Chapter 12](#ch12) (`plan -detailed-exitcode` per root, alert on 2) is the detector; the decision from [Chapter 6](#ch6) is the resolver - `apply -refresh-only` to accept reality when the console change was *right*, a normal apply to restore intent when it was *wrong*, and a `lifecycle.ignore_changes` ([Chapter 5](#ch5)) when some attribute is legitimately owned by another system. The pattern that ends drift arguments: every convergence decision is recorded as one of those three, and "someone changed it in the console" is never the end of the story.

## 14.2 Replacement Without Downtime: The Zero-Downtime Playbook

Plans full of `-/+` are safe in labs and dangerous in production; the playbook for replacements is what makes Terraform production-grade. The core move is `create_before_destroy = true` ([Chapter 5](#ch5)) plus the platform's traffic or replica mechanics: instances join the load balancer before their predecessors leave; containers run two-at-once behind a proxy; databases are replicated before the primary is retired. For resources that cannot coexist (a DNS name, a fixed port), the *rename-first* pattern applies: provision the replacement under a new address, shift traffic, then retire the old - two applies, no outage. Layered on top: health checks as postconditions ([Chapter 11](#ch11)) so a bad replacement fails the plan, and `prevent_destroy` on the objects whose loss is an incident rather than a rollback. When a plan *must* rebuild something that cannot rebuild safely, the answer is not courage; it is reshaping the design until the plan is safe.

## 14.3 Refactoring Living Infrastructure: `moved` and Friends

Production estates are refactored constantly - modules extracted ([Chapter 8](#ch8)), resources renamed, counts converted to `for_each` ([Chapter 3](#ch3)) - and none of it may touch real infrastructure. The declarative toolkit makes refactoring a reviewed diff: `moved` blocks re-address objects across renames, module extractions, and count-to-for_each migrations; `import` blocks ([Chapter 7](#ch7)) adopt strays; `removed` blocks unmanage deliberately. The count-to-`for_each` move is the bookend most estates eventually need, and the recipe is mechanical: add `for_each` keyed by the stable identity, add one `moved` block per existing index (`moved { from = aws_instance.api[0]  to = aws_instance.api["web"] }`), plan until the diff reads **zero changes**, then delete the `moved` blocks after the change has rolled everywhere. That last step - moved blocks are scaffolding, removed once consumers have all caught up - is the part teams forget and then wonder why their diffs have archaeology in them.

## 14.4 Operating at Scale: Speed, Partition, and Boundaries

At hundreds of resources, the friction is *time*; at thousands, it is *coordination*. The time levers: state partitioning ([Chapter 7](#ch7)) so plans refresh less, `-minimal-refresh` (1.17) so even wide plans refresh only what changes, `-parallelism` tuned for provider rate limits, and `refresh = false` in pipelines that just ran a drift check. The coordination levers are organizational: one root per team per service (the blast-radius rule), golden modules ([Chapter 8](#ch8)) so teams consume vetted shapes, policy gates ([Chapter 11](#ch11)) so vetting is enforced rather than remembered, and - the quiet superpower - *narrow interfaces*: a module whose contract is five inputs reviews in a minute, and a change that reviews in a minute ships in an afternoon. The failure mode at scale is never HCL syntax; it is a graph so wide that every plan is everyone's problem, and the fixes above all shrink graphs.

## 14.5 Provider Actions and the Workflow's Expanding Edge

The workflow's newest edge, introduced experimentally around Terraform 1.12 and maturing through the 1.16-1.17 line, is the **`action` block**: provider-defined operations invoked *outside* the create/update/destroy lifecycle - "rotate this credential", "trigger this failover", "clear this cache" - planned and applied like everything else instead of living in console clicking or one-off scripts. Actions are provider-dependent and still settling, but the direction is worth orienting on: operations that used to justify break-glass consoles are migrating into the same reviewed, state-tracked, policy-gated workflow as everything else in this book. Estates that keep a "things we only do in the console" list should revisit it yearly; the list shrinks.

## 14.6 Upgrade Discipline and the OpenTofu Question

Two operational disciplines close the book. *Upgrades*: pin per root ([Chapter 2](#ch2)), upgrade Terraform minor-by-minor with a full plan read per root, upgrade providers in their own PRs ([Chapter 4](#ch4)), and let CI run both `terraform` and `tofu` in lockstep if OpenTofu compatibility is a goal - the CLI grammar is shared, so the CI delta is a second binary, and the failure it catches is the rare divergence worth catching early. *Coexistence*: estates standardizing on one distribution succeed by deciding at the *platform* layer ([Chapter 13](#ch13)) and keeping the HCL portable - which this book's examples have been throughout. The 1.x language core has been a genuinely stable base for a decade of infrastructure change; the craft that this book has built - read the plan, partition the state, contract the module, gate the change - is what makes that base production-grade.
